Multi-Robot Active Cooperative Perception

Perceptive exploitation for support

Can robots parked as lookouts on the paths into a forestry mulching site warn the mulcher of an approaching pedestrian earlier than the mulcher's own lidar?

A forestry mulcher carries a lidar on its roof, but its cutting head obstructs much of the forward view, and the detection rule identifies a pedestrian only at short range. This study tests whether small ground robots, equipped with the same lidar and parked as lookouts on the paths that lead into the site, give earlier warning. All sensors observed every simulated trial at the same time, so the mulcher alone and the mulcher with its lookouts are compared on identical trials.

The site

The site is a simulated oak forest of 160 m radius on flat ground, with 83–85 trees per hectare spaced at least 6.5 m apart. The mulcher stands at the centre, and paths 3 m wide are cleared through the trees. The mulcher's body and cutting head are modelled as static boxes with the dimensions of the real machine, and a Velodyne VLP-16 lidar is mounted on its roof at a height of 2.1 m. Within ±30° of the machine's forward direction, the cutting head obstructs 15 of the lidar's 16 laser channels.

Two site layouts were tested. In the two-entry site, a single path crosses the site, so pedestrians can approach from either end; there is a lookout at each entry. In the three-entry site, three paths lead to the mulcher, each with its own lookout.

Placement of the lookouts

Each lookout's position was computed from the map of the site before any trial. A candidate position lies beside its path, 12–28 m from the mulcher, with no tree trunk within 3.7 m, and must keep a radio link to the mulcher. The simulated radio has a range limit of 30 m, which caps the distance at 28 m. Among the candidates, the chosen position is the one from which an approaching pedestrian is first seen farthest from the mulcher, that is, within 12.5 m of the lookout and with no trunk blocking the line of sight.

  • Four of the five lookouts are 28 m from the mulcher.
  • Lookout B of the two-entry site is 24 m from the mulcher, because every free position farther out along its path has a trunk within 3.7 m.

Each lookout is a Clearpath Husky ground robot carrying the same VLP-16 lidar as the mulcher (range 0.5–100 m, 2 cm range noise, 1800 × 16 points per scan, 10 scans per second). It stays parked, motionless, at its position.

The trials

In each trial, a model pedestrian (a 1.74 m human figure in mid-stride) walks along a path toward the mulcher in 0.5 m steps at a walking pace of 1.3 m/s.

  • Start and end: each trial starts 45 m (±5 m) before the lookout boundary, defined below, and ends 10 m from the mulcher.
  • Design: for each site, 12 mulcher headings (0° to 330° in 30° steps) × each entry × 2 repetitions. This gives 48 trials for the two-entry site and 72 for the three-entry site.
  • Procedure: the simulation is paused between steps. After each step it advances by one lidar period (0.1 s), and each lidar's first scan recorded after the step is used.

Detection and outcome measures

The detection rule was specified before the experiment:

  • Background: the median range of each laser beam over 10 scans without a pedestrian.
  • New return: a return at least 0.3 m closer than the background.
  • Clusters: new returns within 0.5 m of one another, in three dimensions, are grouped together.
  • Alarm: a cluster of at least 5 points on at least 2 laser rings, present in two consecutive scans with centroids within 1 m of each other.

An alarm is a correct detection if its centroid lies within 1 m of the pedestrian; any other alarm is a false alarm. The combined system raises an alarm as soon as the mulcher or any lookout does. A lookout's alarm reaches the mulcher at once, because every lookout is within radio range.

Lookout boundary
The circle around the mulcher at the distance of the path's lookout: 28 m, or 24 m on the path of Lookout B in the two-entry site. The primary outcome is whether the pedestrian is detected before reaching it.
50 m
A second, more demanding outcome: detection while the pedestrian is still more than 50 m from the mulcher.
Warning time
The time from the first alarm until the pedestrian reaches the lookout boundary. A negative value means that the alarm came after the pedestrian had crossed it.
Alternative rules
Declared before any trial, to show how much the result depends on the rule itself:
  • horizontal clustering: the same rule, with the 0.5 m grouping measured in the horizontal plane only;
  • ideal detector: at least 5 lidar points on the pedestrian in a single scan, counted from the simulator's ground truth. No real rule can do better.
Control trials
Twelve trials per site were repeated without lookouts: once with the lookouts moved 1 km away, and once in a simulation in which they were never created. These test whether the presence of the lookouts changes what the mulcher itself detects.

Results

The lookouts give substantially earlier warning. Under the specified rule, the mulcher alone first raised an alarm when the pedestrian was about 14 m away, in every case after the pedestrian had crossed the lookout boundary. With the lookouts, every pedestrian was detected before the boundary, about 40 m from the mulcher. On the same trial, the first alarm came a median of about 22 s earlier, and never less than 20 s earlier.

Interpretation

  • The mulcher's 0 % is partly a property of the rule. The specified rule groups returns within 0.5 m in three dimensions, and the lidar's laser rings are 2° apart. Beyond about 14.3 m, returns on neighbouring rings are more than 0.5 m apart, so no cluster can span the two rings that the rule requires. The rule therefore cannot fire beyond about 14 m from any lidar (12.5 m in an open-ground calibration, for both mountings), and every lookout boundary lies farther out. The same limit explains why no sensor detected a pedestrian beyond 50 m: a lookout 28 m from the mulcher first detects a pedestrian at about 42 m. The same-trial advance of the first alarm is therefore the fairer comparison.
  • Under less restrictive rules the mulcher performs better, and the lookouts better still. With horizontal clustering or the ideal detector, the mulcher alone detects 54–79 % of pedestrians before the boundary, with a median warning time of 1–8 s. With the lookouts, every pedestrian is detected before the boundary and beyond 50 m, with a median warning time of 27–29 s.
  • The lookouts cover the mulcher's blind sector. Every trial that the mulcher alone never detected approached within 45° of its forward direction, where the cutting head obstructs its lidar (bearing taken with the pedestrian 20 m away). In the three-entry site, the mulcher missed all 18 trials that approached within 39° of its forward direction; the lookouts detected every one of them.
  • Each lookout covers one entry. In every trial, the first alarm came from the lookout on the pedestrian's own path (24 trials per lookout). With any one lookout removed, the three-entry site detects only 67 % of pedestrians before the boundary.
  • The lookouts do not alter what the mulcher detects. In the control trials, the mulcher's first alarm occurred at the same 0.5 m step as in the matching trial in 23 of 24 trials per site, and within one step in all 24.

Limitations

  • Only approaches along the paths were tested. The lookouts watch the paths, and every simulated pedestrian used one. A pedestrian approaching through the trees, away from any path, would not pass a lookout; this case was not tested.
  • The lookout positions depend on the radio model. Lookouts are at most 28 m from the mulcher because the simulated radio has a hard range limit of 30 m and a loss of 70 dB per intervening trunk. These are deliberately conservative settings, not measured radio performance: a real link might permit positions farther out, and so more warning, or fail to reach these. Every lookout position had a radio link in the model, and the radio model was not changed.
  • The lookouts were parked at precomputed positions. The positions were computed from a perfect map of the site, the list of tree trunks. In a real mission the robots would first explore the site and then drive to their positions, which was outside the scope of this study. The lookouts were modelled as static bodies, because a parked Husky rocked on its wheels in this simulator and each rocking motion was detected as a person.
  • The pedestrian and the forest are idealised. A single mid-stride human model moves along the path at a constant pace. The forest consists of trunks only, without branches, canopy or undergrowth, on flat ground. Lidar noise is Gaussian with a standard deviation of 2 cm, and lidar poses are exact.
  • Two layouts, with one forest each. The confidence intervals should be read as indicative only.
